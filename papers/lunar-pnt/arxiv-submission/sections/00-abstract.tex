Lunar positioning, navigation, and timing (PNT) is being built. ESA's
Moonlight/LCNS service \citep{esa_moonlight,telespazio2024moonlight}, a
NovaMoon-class geodetic-timing-differential reference station
\citep{molli2026novamoon}, the LunaNet/IOAG interoperability framework
\citep{nasa2025lnis}, and a now-defined Coordinated Lunar Time
\citep{iau2024lcrs,ashby2024lunartime} all reduce to the same engineering core:
determine the orbits and clocks of the lunar infrastructure, and tie them to a
terrestrial or inertial frame. That puts an old estimation question in a new
place. Which measurements make which states observable? We work through one case
of it in an open, reproducible simulator
\citep{iiyama2023lupnt,baweja2026kshana}. In a snapshot batch fit of a small
lunar PNT network, the lunar-local observables (station-to-satellite ranging,
inter-satellite ranging, and a station clock-sync) pin down the network's
internal geometry but barely constrain the surface station's absolute 3-D
position. A single Earth-baseline geodetic Very Long Baseline Interferometry
(VLBI) delay to the lunar beacon fixes that. Over a 200-seed truth/noise
ensemble the median station 3-D error drops from about 793~m without VLBI to
about 13~m with it, a median factor of \textbf{91} (interquartile range 38 to
208). The gain comes from information, not from extra observations: hold the
observation set fixed and coarsen the VLBI delay precision, and the median error
climbs back toward the range-only floor. It is also specific. VLBI sharpens the
absolute positions and the satellite clocks, but the station clock, already
pinned by clock-sync, hardly moves. A Fisher-information analysis turns the
empirical contrast into an observability statement: without an Earth baseline the
lunar frame's absolute datum lies in the null space of the information matrix---a
six-dimensional defect that closes to zero only at three Earth stations---and the
resulting Cram\'er--Rao bound on the station's absolute position
(\SI{20.1}{\meter} for the default network) is attained by the estimator to an
efficiency of $1.00$, so the $91\times$ gain is the information-theoretic optimum
rather than a solver artefact. The same bound, read off against baseline count,
is a closed-form sizing rule. None of this is hypothetical: ESA's CM25-adopted NovaMoon
station carries an Earth-baseline VLBI transmitter of its own
\citep{molli2026novamoon}, and the first GNSS fix on the lunar surface (LuGRE,
2025) put real range-only absolute positioning at the tens-to-hundreds-of-metres
level \citep{lugre2026}. Every figure here is deterministic and rebuilds from a
committed scenario, seed list, and engine version, and each is labelled validated
or modelled. It is a closed-loop observability study rather than a comparison
against real VLBI or ranging data, and we treat that as the headline caveat, not
a footnote. The toolkit behind it (lunar time, VLBI, joint OD and clock, frame
realisation, service-volume integrity, differential PNT, and CCSDS/LunaNet
export) is released open-core.
